1. General Introduction & Periodic Trends
Groups 13 to 18

Position: Groups 13–18 in the p-block of the modern periodic table. The p-subshell is progressively filled with electrons from 1 to 6.

General Valence-Shell Electronic Configuration:   ns2 np1–6 (Exception: Helium is 1s2, placed in Group 18 due to closed-shell noble gas characteristics)
A. General Periodic Trends Summary
Property Across a Period (→) Down a Group (↓)
Atomic & Ionic Radius Generally decreases (effective nuclear charge Zeff increases) Increases (additional electron shells added)
Ionisation Enthalpy Generally increases (smaller size, higher pull on valence e−) Generally decreases (shielding effect dominates)
Electronegativity Generally increases across period Generally decreases down group
Metallic Character Decreases (elements transition to non-metals) Increases (easier loss of valence electrons)
Non-Metallic Character Increases across the period Decreases down the group
B. Oxidation States & Inert Pair Effect

p-Block elements exhibit variable oxidation states because both outermost ns and np electrons participate in chemical bonding. The maximum oxidation state equals the total number of valence electrons (Group number − 10).

💡 The Inert Pair Effect: As we descend the group, the intervening d and f orbitals shield the nuclear charge very poorly. Consequently, the outermost ns2 electron pair is held strongly by the nucleus and shows reluctance to unpair and participate in bonding.
  • Group 13: +1 state stability increases down group (Tl+ > Tl3+).
  • Group 14: +2 state stability increases down group (Pb2+ > Pb4+).
  • Group 15: +3 state stability increases down group (Bi3+ > Bi5+).
  • Group 16: +4 state stability increases down group (Te4+, Po4+ > +6).
C. Anomalous Behaviour of First Member of Each Group

The first element of each group (B, C, N, O, F) differs significantly from its heavier congeners due to five primary factors:

Fundamental Structural Reasons
  1. Extremely small atomic & ionic radius.
  2. High electronegativity & high ionisation enthalpy.
  3. Absence of vacant d-orbitals in valence shell (maximum covalency strictly restricted to 4).
Chemical Consequences
  1. High tendency for pπ–pπ multiple bonding with itself (C=C, C≡C, N≡N) and with other small atoms (C=O, C=N, N=O).
  2. Heavier elements can expand their octet using vacant d-orbitals (e.g., [AlF6]3−, SF6, PCl5 exist, while BF63−, OF6 do not).
2. Group 13 — Boron Family (Icosagens)
ns2 np1

Elements: Boron (B), Aluminium (Al), Gallium (Ga), Indium (In), Thallium (Tl).  |  Oxidation States: +3 and +1.

A. Critical Trends & Anomalies
  • Metallic character: Boron is a typical non-metal/metalloid; Al, Ga, In, Tl are true metals.
  • Oxidation state stability: +3 stability decreases down group; +1 increases down group (Tl+ is highly stable, while Tl3+ acts as a strong oxidising agent).
⚠️ NEET CRITICAL EXCEPTION — Atomic Radius of Gallium vs Aluminium:
Atomic radius of Gallium (135 pm) is slightly SMALLER than Aluminium (143 pm).
Reason: Gallium is preceded by ten 3d-transition elements. The 10 d-electrons exert very poor shielding effect on the outer valence electrons, increasing the effective nuclear charge (Zeff) and pulling the valence shell inward.

C. Key Compounds of Group 13
1. Borax — Na2B4O7·10H2O
  • White crystalline solid; formula contains [B4O5(OH)4]2− units.
  • Aqueous solution is alkaline due to hydrolysis of the weak acid:
    Na2B4O7 + 7H2O → 2NaOH + 4H3BO3
  • Borax Bead Test: On strong heating, swells, loses water, and forms a transparent glassy bead of sodium metaborate + B2O3:
    Na2B4O7·10H2O →Δ Na2B4O7 →Δ 2NaBO2 + B2O3 (glassy bead)
2. Orthoboric Acid — H3BO3 / B(OH)3
  • Layered planar structure with [BO3] units linked by hydrogen bonds.
  • NEET Highlight: Weak Monobasic Lewis Acid! It does not donate H+ directly; instead, it accepts OH− from water:
    B(OH)3 + 2H2O ⇔ [B(OH)4]− + H3O+
  • Thermal Dehydration:
    H3BO3 →100°C HBO2 (Metaboric) →Δ B2O3 (Boron trioxide)
3. Diborane — B2H6
  • Electron-deficient hydride; each B is sp3-hybridised.
  • Bonding: 4 terminal 2-centre 2-electron (2c–2e) B–H bonds + 2 bridging 3-centre 2-electron (3c–2e) "banana bonds".
  • Combustion is highly exothermic (rocket fuel potential):
    B2H6 + 3O2 → B2O3 + 3H2O  ΔH = −1976 kJ/mol
4. Aluminium Chloride — AlCl3
  • Electron-deficient Lewis acid; acts as catalyst in Friedel-Crafts reaction.
  • Anhydrous AlCl3 achieves stability by forming a dimer Al2Cl6 in vapour/solid state via dative coordinate bonds.
  • In aqueous solution, it forms hydrated ions: [Al(H2O)6]3+ + 3Cl−.
💡 NEET High-Yield: Lewis Acidity Order of Boron Trihalides:
BF3 < BCl3 < BBr3 < BI3   (BI3 is the STRONGEST Lewis acid)
Mechanism: In BF3, effective 2pπ–2pπ back-bonding occurs between filled 2p of Fluorine and vacant 2p of Boron, significantly reducing electron deficiency. As halogen size increases (3p in Cl, 4p in Br, 5p in I), orbital overlap becomes ineffective, so back-bonding diminishes and Lewis acidity sharply increases.
3. Group 14 — Carbon Family (Crystallogens)
ns2 np2

Elements: Carbon (C), Silicon (Si), Germanium (Ge), Tin (Sn), Lead (Pb).  |  Oxidation States: +4, +2 (and −4 for C).

A. Catenation & Allotropy

Catenation: The unique ability of an atom to link with other atoms of the same element through strong covalent bonds.

Catenation Order:   C >> Si > Ge ≈ Sn > Pb

Carbon exhibits exceptionally high catenation due to its small size and high C–C bond dissociation enthalpy (348 kJ/mol).

C. Carbon Oxides: CO vs CO2
Carbon Monoxide (CO) — Neutral Oxide
  • Colourless, odourless, highly poisonous gas.
  • Binds to haemoglobin forming carboxyhaemoglobin (~300× more stable than oxyhaemoglobin), blocking O2 transport.
  • Powerful reducing agent in metallurgy:
    Fe2O3 + 3CO → 2Fe + 3CO2
Carbon Dioxide (CO2) — Acidic Oxide
  • Linear molecule; carbon is sp-hybridised (μ = 0).
  • Forms carbonic acid (H2CO3) with water.
  • Lime water test & excess bicarbonate reaction:
    Ca(OH)2 + CO2 → CaCO3↓ (milky) + H2O
    CaCO3 + H2O + CO2 (excess) → Ca(HCO3)2 (clear)
  • Solid CO2 is called Dry Ice (sublimes at −78°C).
D. Silicon Compounds: Silicates, Zeolites & Silicones

1. Silicon Dioxide (SiO2): Giant covalent 3D network solid. Each Si atom is tetrahedrally bonded to four Oxygen atoms, and each Oxygen atom is shared between two Si atoms. (High melting point ~1700°C, chemically very inert).

2. Silicates Classification: Basic structural building block is the tetrahedral [SiO4]4− ion.

Silicate Type Shared Corners / Oxygen Basic Formula Unit Mineral Example
Orthosilicates 0 (isolated tetrahedra) [SiO4]4− Phenacite (Be2SiO4), Zircon
Pyrosilicates (Island) 1 oxygen shared between 2 units [Si2O7]6− Thortveitite (Sc2Si2O7)
Cyclic (Ring) Silicates 2 oxygen atoms per tetrahedron (SiO3)n2n− e.g. [Si3O9]6− Beryl (Be3Al2Si6O18)

3D Framework Silicates All 4 oxygen atoms shared (SiO2)n (neutral framework) Quartz, Feldspar, Zeolites
💡 Zeolites & ZSM-5 (High-Yield NEET Fact):
Zeolites are microporous crystalline hydrated aluminosilicates. In the 3D-SiO2 network, some Si4+ atoms are replaced by Al3+, generating a net negative framework charge balanced by cations like Na+, K+, Ca2+.
  • Water Softening: Cations exchange with hard water Ca2+ and Mg2+ ions.
  • Shape-Selective Catalysis: Pores and channels trap specific size reactant molecules.
  • ZSM-5: A specialised zeolite catalyst used to convert alcohols directly into gasoline (petrol) via dehydration.

3. Silicones: Synthetic organosilicon polymers with repeating −R2Si−O−SiR2− linkages. Prepared by hydrolysis of alkyl/aryl chlorosilanes (R2SiCl2) followed by condensation polymerisation. Properties: Water-repellent, high thermal stability, electrical insulators, used as lubricants and surgical implants.

B. Industrial Processes: Haber Process & Ostwald Process
Haber Process (Ammonia — NH3)
N2(g) + 3H2(g) ⇔ 2NH3(g) ΔH = −92.4 kJ
  • Optimum Conditions: High pressure (~200 atm), temperature (~700 K).
  • Catalyst: Finely divided Iron with promoter Molybdenum (or K2O + Al2O3).
Ostwald Process (Nitric Acid — HNO3)
  • Step 1: 4NH3 + 5O2 →[Pt-Rh, 500K] 4NO + 6H2O
  • Step 2: 2NO + O2 ⇔ 2NO2
  • Step 3: 3NO2 + H2O → 2HNO3 + NO
⚠️ NEET TRAP — Reactions of Copper with Nitric Acid:
3Cu + 8HNO3 (dilute) → 3Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO↑ (Nitric Oxide) + 4H2O
Cu + 4HNO3 (concentrated) → Cu(NO3)2 + 2NO2↑ (Nitrogen Dioxide) + 2H2O
Crucial: HNO3 is a strong oxidising agent and never liberates H2 gas with metals (except very dilute acid with Mg and Mn).

D. Phosphorus Halides & Oxoacids (PCl3, PCl5 & Acids)

Phosphorus Pentachloride (PCl5): In the gaseous and liquid phase, it has a trigonal bipyramidal structure.
NEET Point: The two axial P–Cl bonds are longer and weaker (240 pm) than the three equatorial P–Cl bonds (202 pm) due to greater bond-pair repulsive strain. Hence, on heating, PCl5 decomposes into PCl3 and Cl2.
In solid state, PCl5 exists as an ionic solid: [PCl4]+[PCl6]− (cation is tetrahedral, anion is octahedral).

E. Master Table: Oxoacids of Phosphorus & Basicity Rule
Golden Rule for Phosphorus Oxoacids: Basicity = Number of P–OH bonds (only OH hydrogens ionise as H+).
Reducing power = Proportional to the number of direct P–H bonds (P–H acts as strong reducing agent).

5. Group 16 — Oxygen Family (Chalcogens)
ns2 np4

Elements: Oxygen (O), Sulphur (S), Selenium (Se), Tellurium (Te), Polonium (Po).  |  Oxidation States: −2, +2, +4, +6.

A. Hydrides of Group 16 (H2E) Trends
Property Observed Order Reasoning
Acidic Strength H2O < H2S < H2Se < H2Te E–H bond dissociation enthalpy decreases as size increases; H+ release becomes easier.
Thermal Stability H2O > H2S > H2Se > H2Te H–E bond strength decreases down the group.
Reducing Power H2O (none) < H2S < H2Se < H2Te Ease of liberation of hydrogen increases down group.
Boiling Point H2S < H2Se < H2Te < H2O H2O has abnormally high b.p. due to extensive intermolecular hydrogen bonding.
B. Oxygen (O2) vs Ozone (O3) & Magnetism
Dioxygen (O2) — Paramagnetic Gas
  • Molecular Orbital Configuration shows two unpaired electrons in anti-bonding π*2px and π*2py orbitals.
  • NEET Trap: O2 is paramagnetic despite having an even number (16) of electrons!
Ozone (O3) — Powerful Oxidant
  • Pale blue gas with angular bent geometry (O–O–O angle = 117°).
  • Thermodynamically unstable; readily decomposes into O2 and nascent oxygen [O]:
    O3 → O2 + [O]   (acts as powerful oxidiser)
C. Allotropes of Sulphur & The Contact Process

Sulphur Allotropes: Two major crystalline forms, both consisting of puckered S8 rings (crown shape):
• Rhombic Sulphur (α-sulphur): Yellow crystals, stable below 369 K.
• Monoclinic Sulphur (β-sulphur): Needle-shaped crystals, stable above 369 K.
Transition Temperature = 369 K: Both forms coexist in equilibrium at this temperature.

Industrial Manufacture of Sulphuric Acid (Contact Process):

Step 1: S + O2 → SO2
Step 2 (Key): 2SO2 + O2 ⇔ 2SO3 Catalyst: V2O5, Temp: 673–720 K, Pressure: 2 bar
Step 3: SO3 + H2SO4 (conc) → H2S2O7 (Oleum / Pyrosulphuric acid)
Step 4: H2S2O7 + H2O → 2H2SO4 (Pure 96–98% concentration)
6. Group 17 — Halogens (Salt Formers)
ns2 np5

Elements: Fluorine (F), Chlorine (Cl), Bromine (Br), Iodine (I), Astatine (At).  |  Oxidation States: −1 (F only −1; Cl, Br, I show −1, +1, +3, +5, +7).

A. Critical Halogen Trends
Oxidising & Reducing Orders
Oxidising Power:   F2 > Cl2 > Br2 > I2
Halide Reducing:   I− > Br− > Cl− > F−

F2 is the strongest chemical oxidising agent known due to high hydration enthalpy and low F–F bond dissociation energy.

Anomalous Bond Dissociation Enthalpy
Bond Enthalpy: Cl2 > Br2 > F2 > I2

NEET Exception: F–F bond enthalpy is unusually lower than Cl–Cl and Br–Br due to strong inter-electronic repulsions between non-bonding lone pairs in small F atom.

B. Hydrogen Halides (HX) Trends
Property Observed Order NEET Key Concept
Acidic Strength HF < HCl < HBr < HI H–X bond dissociation energy decreases sharply from HF to HI. HI is strongest acid.
Thermal Stability HF > HCl > HBr > HI H–X bond strength decreases down the group.
Reducing Nature HF < HCl < HBr < HI HI readily donates hydrogen; strong reducing agent.
Boiling Point HCl < HBr < HI < HF HF has highest b.p. due to strong intermolecular hydrogen bonding.

C. Applications of Noble Gases
Gas Crucial Applications
Helium (He) Filling weather observation balloons (non-flammable, light); cryogenic agent for MRI superconducting magnets; diluent for oxygen in deep-sea diving (low blood solubility).
Neon (Ne) Discharge tubes and glow signs (beacon lights) for botanical gardens and airport runway signals.
Argon (Ar) Inert atmosphere in high-temperature metallurgical arc welding and filling electric incandescent light bulbs.
Krypton / Xenon High-intensity photographic flash tubes, lasers, and specialized surgical equipment.
Radon (Rn) Radioactive tracer and radiotherapy treatment of malignant tumours (cancer).
